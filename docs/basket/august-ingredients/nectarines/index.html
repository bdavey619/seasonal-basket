August · San Diego

Nectarines

Nectarines are peaches without the fuzz, and in August they often hit their stride as peach season winds down. The flavor is similar — sweet, aromatic, deeply summery — but the skin is thinner and doesn't need peeling. This is the last good stone fruit window before fall. It is also the month when the fruit has traveled the furthest and been kept the coldest, which is why choosing well matters more now than it did in July.

How to choose it

  • Look at the background color, not the red blush — the gold or cream under the blush should have no green left in it. Red is variety and sun exposure, and it tells you nothing
  • Press the shoulder near the stem, not the cheek. The skin is thin enough to feel soft over flesh that isn't
  • Heavy for its size. A light nectarine was picked before it filled out
  • Buy from an unrefrigerated display when the store has one. Stone fruit chilled while still firm turns mealy and never recovers
  • Fragrance confirms; it doesn't filter. Cold fruit doesn't smell like anything, so a quiet nectarine with gold shoulders is still worth taking home
  • Avoid green shoulders and rock-hard fruit — that one was picked too early and will only soften, never sweeten

Buy this much: 4–5 nectarines for the week. They don't all need to be ready on the same day.

Firm is fine. Green is not. Take home nectarines that are heavy and gold-shouldered even if they don't give yet, then leave them on the counter for two or three days — stem side down, out of direct sun, in a paper bag if you want to hurry them. They will soften, get juicy, and start to smell like something. What they will not do is get sweeter than the day they were picked, which is why the color under the blush decides the purchase and the give only decides the day you eat it.

Stone fruit is still affordable in August, though supply thins by late in the month and more of it arrives from further away. Buy them when you see good ones — the window doesn't come back.

During the week

  • Sliced on Greek yogurt in the morning
  • Eaten as a snack, ripe
  • Sliced alongside the weekend cheese plate

On a weekend

Alongside the fig and burrata plate — or in place of figs if they weren't available. The combination of ripe stone fruit, good cheese, and cured meat holds up in either direction.

One thing worth learning

The dry, cottony nectarine has a name: internal breakdown, or woolliness. It happens when stone fruit is held cold — roughly 36°F to 46°F — while it is still firm, which describes almost every refrigerated grocery display in the second half of summer. The damage is done before you get there and cannot be undone on your counter. This is the whole reason a nectarine bought in August can look flawless, feel perfect, and taste like nothing. Buy from the room-temperature stack when there is one, ripen at home, and refrigerate only at the end.

Good with this month

Basil, Figs

Good with your staples

Greek yogurt, chicken thighs, prosciutto

Storage

Counter until ripe, stem side down, in a single layer. Refrigerate only once it's fully soft and fragrant, and eat within a day or two after that. A firm nectarine put in the refrigerator is the one that comes out dry and cottony.
