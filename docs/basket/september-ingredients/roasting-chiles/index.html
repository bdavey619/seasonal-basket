September · San Diego

Roasting chiles

Poblanos, Anaheims, and Hatch chiles come in together in late August and are gone by mid-October. This is the short window when they are everywhere, cheap, and often being roasted in a drum outside the market. Nothing else in the year replaces them.

How to choose it

  • Firm, glossy, and heavy for their size
  • Flat-ish sides char more evenly than deeply lobed ones
  • Poblano for stuffing — wide shoulders, thick walls
  • Anaheim or Hatch for strips — longer, thinner, milder
  • Skip anything wrinkled or light; it has been sitting

Buy this much: 6–8 for the week. They lose about half their volume once roasted and peeled, so this is less than it looks.

Cheapest they will be all year, and cheaper still by the sack. If a market is roasting them on site, that price usually beats buying them raw and doing it yourself.

During the week

  • Strips folded into a rice bowl
  • Strips in tacos with escabeche and lime
  • Blended with cream for pasta
  • Chopped into scrambled eggs

On a weekend

Chiles rellenos: charred, peeled, stuffed with jack cheese, baked, and set in a simple tomato broth.

One thing worth learning

Charring a chile is not about cooking it. It is about destroying the skin, which is thin, papery, and unpleasant to eat, so that what is underneath can be. That is also why the covered fifteen minutes after charring matters more than the char itself: the trapped steam separates skin from flesh. Skip the steam and you will spend ten minutes scraping. Do it, and the skin slips off in sheets.

Good with this month

Tomatillos, Cilantro, Limes, Delicata squash

Good with your staples

chicken thighs, ground beef or turkey, beans, eggs, sticky white rice

Storage

Whole and raw, a week in the crisper. Roasted and peeled, four days in the fridge in a covered container, or freeze them in strips — they freeze better than almost any other vegetable in this basket.

House Flavor

  • Escabeche
