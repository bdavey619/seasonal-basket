Escabeche

One jar. Three weeks of acid and crunch.

Make one jar

  • 2 Anaheim chiles or 4 jalapeños, cut into rounds
  • 2 carrots, sliced on an angle
  • ½ white onion, sliced thick
  • 3 garlic cloves, peeled and smashed
  • 1 cup white or apple cider vinegar
  • 1 cup water
  • 1 tbsp kosher salt
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 2 bay leaves, a pinch of dried oregano, a few peppercorns
  • 1 tbsp neutral oil

Warm the oil in a saucepan over medium heat. Add the onion, carrot, garlic, and chiles and cook for about three minutes — just until the edges soften and the kitchen starts to smell like it. Add the vinegar, water, salt, sugar, and the bay, oregano, and peppercorns. Bring to a simmer, then take it off the heat immediately. Pour everything, brine included, into a jar. Cool on the counter, then refrigerate. It's good in an hour and better on day three. The ratio underneath this is the part worth keeping: one cup vinegar, one cup water, one tablespoon salt, one teaspoon sugar. That brine works on any crisp vegetable you have too much of.

Keeps in the refrigerator for three weeks, and the brine outlasts the vegetables. Don't throw the brine out — it belongs on beans and in dressings.

Use it this week

Rice bowl Spoon it on top.

Add a little brine with it. Rice takes acid better than people expect.

Tacos Pile it on.

This is the jar that makes a home taco taste like it came from somewhere. Go heavier than feels right.

Beans Stir in the brine.

A spoonful of brine into a pot of beans does what a squeeze of vinegar does, with more going on.

Chicken thighs Serve alongside.

Something crisp and sharp next to something rich and browned. That's the whole idea.

A sandwich Chop it fine.

Chopped escabeche does the job of pickles, mustard, and hot sauce at once.
