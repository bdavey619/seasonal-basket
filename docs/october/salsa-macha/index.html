Salsa macha

One jar. The poblano, dried, fried, and kept.

Make one jar

  • 1 cup neutral oil
  • 6 garlic cloves, thinly sliced
  • ⅓ cup roasted peanuts
  • 2 tbsp sesame seeds
  • 3 ancho chiles, stemmed, seeded, and torn into pieces
  • 6–10 chiles de árbol, stemmed (fewer if you're careful)
  • 1 tsp kosher salt
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 1 tbsp apple cider vinegar

Put the oil, garlic, and peanuts in a small saucepan over medium-low heat. Cook gently, stirring now and then, until the garlic is pale gold and crisp, about eight minutes — not brown. Add the sesame seeds for one minute. Take the pan off the heat, then add the chiles and stir: the oil is hot enough to toast them without burning them, and burnt chiles are the one way to ruin this. Let it sit five minutes. Pour everything into a blender with the salt, sugar, and vinegar, and pulse a few times — it should stay chunky, like gravel in oil, not smooth. Cool completely, then jar it. The idea underneath is worth keeping: fry the aromatics gently, take it off the heat, and let the leftover heat toast the chiles. It works with any dried chile you find.

Keep it in the fridge, with the solids under the oil. It's best in the first two weeks and good for a month. The oil firms up cold; let it sit out ten minutes before using. Always use a clean spoon.

Use it this week

Rice bowl Spoon it on top.

With some of the oil. Rice soaks it up, and the peanuts stay crunchy.

Tacos Instead of salsa.

Sweet potato and poblano need something with crunch and heat. This is both.

Beans Stir it in.

A spoonful and its oil turns a can of beans into a bowl you'd order.

The weekend braise Set it on the table.

Rich, slow-cooked chicken wants something sharp and crunchy beside it. Let everyone add their own.

Eggs Over the top.

Fried egg, salsa macha, a tortilla. Breakfast is done.
